The Interference Effect

A Critical Review from Foundational Theory to Modern Practice

March 2025

The global popularity of fitness competitions such as HYROX and CrossFit has led to more people training to demonstrate high levels of both strength and endurance (Fernández-Navarrete et al., 2025). This has given rise to the term "hybrid athlete," which describes an individual who works to improve both of these physical capacities at the same time through what is known as concurrent training. As a result, the discussion around the interference effect has grown in importance. At its core, this theory refers to the potential trade-off where combining strength and endurance training may impair gains in muscle size, strength, and power compared with resistance training performed alone (Nader, 2006). Consequently, training programmes and recommendations often portray endurance training as detrimental to any strength, power, and hypertrophy gains. However, the clear success of modern hybrid athletes, who show very high standards in both strength and endurance tasks, calls into question how relevant the interference effect really is and demands a critical re-evaluation. To understand this topic, this essay will begin by reviewing the early research that established the interference effect. It will then explore more recent studies that provide a real-world perspective on the effect. Finally, it will offer practical advice on how to programme strength and endurance training together effectively.

Foundations of the Interference Effect

The scientific basis for the interference effect can be traced back to the foundational work of Robert C. Hickson. In his seminal 1980 study, he demonstrated that training simultaneously for strength and endurance reduced the capacity to develop strength when compared to strength training alone (Hickson, 1980). However, the study's design reveals a significant confounding variable from a modern perspective, as the concurrent group was exposed to what would now be considered an excessive training volume. While the strength-only group trained their lower body five days a week, the combined group performed six weekly endurance sessions in addition to the same five strength sessions. This immense difference in workload, totalling 11 sessions per week for the combined group, suggests that the observed interference may largely be attributable to overtraining and cumulative fatigue, rather than a direct physiological conflict.

Kraemer et al. (1995) employed a comparable study design to examine the hormonal responses linked to concurrent and resistance-only training programmes. Their findings showed that the resistance training group experienced reductions in cortisol concentrations and a more favourable testosterone-to-cortisol ratio. This created a more anabolic environment, which is associated with increases in strength and muscle mass (Uchida et al., 2004). By contrast, the concurrent training group displayed elevated cortisol levels and an unfavourable hormonal profile, suggesting a more catabolic physiological environment that may impair strength and hypertrophy adaptations (De Luccia, 2016). However, it is debatable whether this hormonal shift was caused by the endurance training itself or by the substantial increase in total workload. Given the similarity of the study design to that of Hickson (1980), the catabolic state of the combined group may also be attributable to the high training volume rather than to the specific inclusion of endurance training.

Consequently, these early studies potentially reflect the negative effects of excessive training volume and recovery demands rather than demonstrating a true physiological interference effect. More recent research supports this suspicion, showing that excessive training stress and insufficient recovery consistently elevate cortisol levels, regardless of the exercise modality (Jin et al., 2015). This hormonal shift is a known consequence of overtraining and creates a catabolic state that is inherently counterproductive to muscle growth and strength adaptation (Mangine et al., 2015).

The Molecular Basis: AMPK and mTOR Conflict

To date, the most compelling evidence supporting the interference effect comes from molecular-level research. On the one hand, there is the enzyme mTOR (Mammalian Target of Rapamycin), which is activated by resistance training and is the master regulator of muscle protein synthesis (Nader, 2006). On the other hand, there is AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), a cellular energy sensor that is activated by endurance training (Bolster et al., 2002). The signalling pathways of these two enzymes, which govern adaptations to strength and endurance training, have been shown to be in direct conflict with each other. Gwinn et al. (2008) report that AMPK activation directly inhibits the mTOR signalling pathway, consequently hindering muscle growth. In essence, the signal to adapt to endurance training actively suppresses the signal to build muscle.

Re-evaluating the Interference Effect: From Theory to Practice

More recent research acknowledges that the interference effect exists, but questions its practical significance and underlying causes. The interaction between these molecular pathways is more nuanced than an "on/off switch". For example, Apró et al. (2013) demonstrated that performing endurance training after resistance training did not result in the inhibition of the crucial mTOR signal. These findings have direct practical implications for practitioners, who should programme resistance training before endurance work within any given session. If separate sessions are possible, allowing several hours of rest between modalities will further reduce the risk of molecular interference.

Furthermore, even if a degree of molecular interference exists, its effect on physical outcome may be minimal. A review by Murach and Bagley (2016) highlights the lack of evidence for a significant interference effect on muscle growth, suggesting its impact is less pronounced than previously believed. In fact, Lundberg et al. (2013) reported comparable muscle mass gains with concurrent training compared to resistance training alone. This suggests that, by adjusting certain training variables, the theoretical interference can be effectively managed in real-world settings. Key variables that have been shown to mitigate interference include the use of low-impact endurance activities such as cycling instead of running, managing total training volume, and allowing adequate recovery time between sessions (Schumann et al., 2022; Wilson et al., 2012).

An alternative hypothesis by Denadai and Greco (2025), which shifts the focus away from molecular inhibition and towards managing residual fatigue, has gained relevance. This model posits that the attenuated gains observed in certain concurrent training studies are not due to direct molecular interference, but are instead the result of residual fatigue. According to the "acute hypothesis", the fatigue induced by a preceding endurance session impairs the quality of the subsequent strength training session by reducing an athlete's ability to produce maximal force. This results in a weaker mechanical stimulus and therefore a blunted anabolic signal. This occurs not because mTOR is being actively blocked by AMPK, but because the initial stimulus required to fully activate it is compromised.

This concept is particularly important for high-level athletes. As an athlete approaches their genetic potential, a greater training intensity is required to stimulate further adaptation. For these athletes, any reduction in training quality due to fatigue can be a significant barrier to progress, so intelligent programming is paramount.

Conclusion: A New Paradigm for Concurrent Training

In conclusion, the interference effect has shifted from an unavoidable molecular conflict model to a far more manageable one. AMPK–mTOR antagonism is real, but its practical significance depends heavily on how training is structured. In most real-world training situations, lower session quality because of residual fatigue appears to be a more significant barrier to progress than direct molecular conflict. For recreational athletes, the theoretical concerns are largely negligible. However, for advanced athletes pushing closer to their limits, the details matter considerably more. Session order, modality selection, recovery and volume all become variables that must be controlled with precision.

Therefore, rather than avoiding concurrent training, high-performance athletes should embrace it with a level of planning appropriate to their training status. Within the same session, resistance training should precede endurance work to protect the anabolic signalling environment. Where possible, high-intensity bouts of each modality should be separated by adequate recovery time to preserve the quality of both stimuli. Where interference is a concern, cycling could be favoured over running as the primary endurance modality, given its lower mechanical demand on the musculature. Above all, total volume must be kept in check. Much of the dramatic interference documented in early research was probably caused by fatigue, and volume is the most controllable variable available to any coach or athlete.

This updated perspective should not be revolutionary for most experienced practitioners, who already understand the importance of managing fatigue and recovery. Instead, this re-evaluation should serve as a strong encouragement to programme concurrent training with confidence and not shy away from it due to outdated fears of the interference effect.
