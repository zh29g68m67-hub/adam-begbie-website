Is Overspeed Training an Effective Method to Improve Maximal Sprint Speed?

A Critical Evaluation of the Evidence

May 2025

Maximal sprint speed (MSS) is considered a key performance indicator (KPI) in athletics and in most other running-based sports, including football, rugby, and American football (Harper et al., 2019; Haugen et al., 2020). In sports played on smaller courts, such as handball and basketball, athletes may not reach MSS. Nevertheless, improving MSS is still considered beneficial, as it can positively impact acceleration performance (Morin et al., 2012). Consequently, dedicated speed training that targets maximal velocity is a fundamental component of an athlete's physical preparation. Sprint training can be categorised into three types: unresisted (free) sprinting, resisted sprinting, and assisted sprinting. While all three methods are utilised, assisted sprint training, commonly known as overspeed (OS) training, is supported by less empirical evidence, with its application often guided by anecdotal reports. Therefore, this essay will critically evaluate the existing scientific literature to examine the current understanding and make recommendations for its practical application.

The Biomechanical Determinants of Maximal Sprint Speed

The expression of MSS is governed by the athlete's stride rate (i.e. the number of strides performed per unit of time) and stride length (i.e. the distance covered per stride) (Mero et al., 1992). Increasing either of these variables through training is generally considered to be the key to improving MSS (Behrens & Simonson, 2011). However, it is important to understand that these are merely outcomes. Effective training must target the correct underlying factors (e.g. neural, morphological, metabolic, anthropometric, and technical) that govern them. For instance, stride length is determined by the force exerted during the stance phase (Salo et al., 2011). Simply encouraging an athlete to reach further without increasing force production can lead to suboptimal mechanics, such as increased braking forces upon foot contact, which may compromise performance.

While the magnitude of force produced during the stance phase primarily determines stride length, stride rate is largely governed by the ability to minimise ground contact time (Salo et al., 2011; Weyand et al., 2000). This, in turn, depends on the athlete's rate of force development (RFD).

The Theory and Methods of Overspeed Training

MSS is often described as an athlete's individual "speed barrier", representing the highest velocity they can reach without assistance. To exceed this limit, an external force is needed to increase the athlete's horizontal velocity. Overspeed (OS) training methods, such as downhill running or sprinting with the assistance of elastic bands or motorised towing systems, provide this external force, enabling athletes to reach supramaximal velocities (K. Clark et al., 2021). It is hypothesised that exposure to velocities above the athlete's natural speed barrier creates a movement-specific overload of the neuromuscular system (K. Clark et al., 2021; Corn & Knudson, 2003). This overload is believed to increase neural activity, leading to improved intramuscular coordination. Consequently, adaptations such as reduced ground contact time and an increased stride rate may occur (Cecilia-Gallego et al., 2022b). Furthermore, the elevated running velocities constrain athletes to generate force within shorter ground contact times. The need to apply force more rapidly may further enhance neuromuscular efficiency, supporting the idea that OS training can increase stride rate and, consequently, improve sprint performance.

A Critical Review of the Evidence

The available research on OS training with towing systems has primarily focused on its acute effects. A key systematic review and meta-analysis by Cecilia-Gallego et al. (2022b) confirms that this method effectively subjects athletes to the intended supramaximal stimulus. Acute kinematic responses include increased stride length and flight time, as well as increased ground reaction force and reduced ground contact. However, the underlying mechanisms driving these changes are unclear. In particular, it is unclear whether the observed increase in ground reaction force reflects an enhanced ability of the athlete to generate propulsive force, or whether it is mechanically induced by the towing system. The latter explanation would be problematic, as higher sprinting velocities are usually associated with reduced horizontal braking forces (Nagahara et al., 2018). Conversely, towing assistance may increase these braking forces, particularly at higher towing loads, which could disrupt optimal sprint mechanics.

This uncertainty raises the broader question of whether the observed kinematic changes represent meaningful neuromuscular adaptations or are simply artefacts of the external assistance. This concern is further reinforced by the lack of evidence for an increase in stride rate (Cecilia-Gallego et al., 2022b; van den Tillaar & Gamble, 2019), which directly contradicts the theoretical rationale behind OS training.

Currently, there is a lack of substantial longitudinal data on the effects of OS training. A pilot study by Cecilia-Gallego et al. (2025) examined the impact of ten OS training sessions performed over a period of five weeks on MSS and various kinematic variables in six young athletes. While statistical significance was not achieved, small improvements in MSS were detected, which could still be practically relevant given the sensitivity of sprint performance to minimal changes. By contrast, Lahti et al. (2020) reported significant improvements in MSS in male rugby players after a 10-week assisted sprint programme. However, as only five out of the ten participants improved, the authors emphasised the importance of tailoring the training load to optimise outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that OS training can induce adaptations within relatively short timeframes, likely through neuromuscular and coordinative mechanisms. However, it is not currently possible to determine whether longer intervention durations lead to greater or more consistent improvements, as there is a lack of longitudinal data beyond 10 weeks.

Practical Applications and Coaching Considerations

Although the current evidence is limited, it provides several key takeaways for the practical application of OS training. Due to the uncertainty in the literature, a cautious approach is recommended, especially with regard to athlete selection. There is general agreement that OS training should only be implemented with athletes who already demonstrate good, stable sprint mechanics (Cecilia-Gallego et al., 2022b). In less experienced athletes, whose sprint mechanics are not yet fully established, OS training may promote movement patterns that are dependent on external assistance, potentially limiting the development of maximal sprinting speed during unassisted running (D. A. Clark et al., 2009).

Methods such as downhill running or elastic resistance can be used to achieve overspeed conditions, but motorised towing systems such as the 1080 Sprint (1080 Motion, Lidingö, Sweden) and the T-Apax (Hunting Technology Limited, Hong Kong, China) are widely regarded as the gold standard. These systems allow precise control of the towing load, thereby enabling a more individualised training approach. However, the optimal loading parameters remain an area of ongoing research. Current recommendations suggest loads of approximately 2–5 kg, corresponding to ~3.5–7% of body mass (Cecília-Gallego et al., 2022a; D. A. Clark et al., 2009). In practice, the applied load should be sufficient to enable the athlete to exceed their natural speed barrier without negatively altering their sprint mechanics (Cecília-Gallego et al., 2022a; van den Tillaar & Gamble, 2019). This highlights the importance of continuous technical monitoring during OS training. Coaches must be able to identify detrimental changes in sprint mechanics, such as excessive forward trunk lean, a braking foot strike positioned ahead of the centre of mass, or reduced pelvic stability.

Conclusion and Future Directions

OS training is a specialised method that aims to enhance MSS by exposing athletes to supramaximal velocities. Theoretically, this stimulus improves neuromuscular function by increasing stride rate. However, the current body of evidence paints a more complex picture. While OS training does produce acute increases in running velocity, these appear to be driven by increases in stride length rather than stride rate. It is important to note that it is currently impossible to determine whether these adaptations reflect meaningful neuromuscular changes or are merely mechanical artefacts resulting from the external towing force.

Despite the limited longitudinal data, preliminary findings suggest that OS training may lead to improvements in MSS over relatively short intervention periods, likely due to neural and coordinative adaptations. However, it is unclear whether these adaptations would be further enhanced by longer interventions. Overall, while OS training shows promise as part of a comprehensive speed development programme, its effectiveness and the mechanisms underlying it require further investigation before definitive guidelines can be established. Future research should prioritise longitudinal study designs that incorporate direct measurement techniques, such as force plates and electromyography, in order to differentiate between passive mechanical effects and genuine neuromuscular adaptations.
