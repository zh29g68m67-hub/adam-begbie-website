A-Skips Are a Waste of Time

April 2025

Walk into the Tuesday speed session at any football club, from the academy level to the professionals, and you will see them. A line of players moving with varying degrees of enthusiasm as they cycle through the familiar sequence: A-skips, B-skips, butt kicks and straight-leg bounds. This has been viewed as the gold standard for teaching sprint mechanics for decades. The underlying logic is that, to run faster, you must deconstruct the sprint into its parts, practise them in isolation and trust that they will come together seamlessly within the actual sprint. But it rarely, if ever, does. Therefore, we must reconsider and search for better solutions. One solution is to replace the entire library of classic sprint drills with a set of eight to twelve mini-hurdles, or wickets. Our obsession with breaking down the sprint has caused us to forget how to simply teach the skill of sprinting. We have become drill collectors when we should be movement coaches.

The traditional approach assumes that athletes can take slow, isolated motor patterns and stitch them together to form efficient sprinting. It's like trying to learn to squat by first practising flexion and extension at the ankle, then the knee, and then the hip, and expecting to squat perfectly without ever performing the complete movement. Sprinting is a highly complex skill. The movements happen so quickly that it is impossible to control them consciously. Whenever a player reaches a new top speed, it looks effortless. The upper body is relaxed while the legs move in an explosive rhythm. Rather than breaking the skill down, the best way to improve it is to provide the athlete with an environment in which they can find a better solution themselves. This is the basis of a constraints-led coaching approach, and wickets are the simplest way to implement it.

Coaching the Same Thing Twice

The problem with a drill-heavy approach is not that the drills are inherently bad, but that they create an inefficient coaching cycle. We have all spent hours teaching an athlete to perform an A-skip with high knees and a toe-up position, only to watch them revert to a low-knee, backside-dominant gait every time the velocity increases. The coach then has to address the same mechanical flaws all over again during the sprint itself. For a S&C coach who has limited time with the team, this is a real problem. Everything we do needs to be as efficient as possible. Therefore, we can't waste time on drills that are performed outside the running context.

Rather than separating the drill from the sprint, the aim is to combine them with a tool that incorporates the desired mechanics into the action itself. That is what wickets do. However, they don't eliminate the need for coaching. If the coach possesses a good coaching eye and is able to cue the right corrections, the whole process becomes considerably more effective. The instruction "stay tall," for example, is an abstract cue to act on during an A-skip. When an athlete is running through a set of wickets, it has immediate physical meaning. They have to stay tall to create the clearance needed to cycle their legs over the hurdles. This frees the coach to offer targeted feedback that refines a movement already happening, rather than trying to construct one from scratch. You coach the skill in an environment that has done the foundational teaching for you.

The Application of Wickets: A Spectrum of Intensity

Wickets can teach maximum velocity mechanics across a full range of intensities. Using a single set of hurdles, a coach can develop the mechanical foundation at low speed and then provide an environment where it can be expressed at full speed. The same set of hurdles covers the whole spectrum.

1. Low-Intensity Application: Dribbles

A practical starting point to the wicket setup is to use your own foot size as a reference and then modify spacing according to how athletes respond. For example, with a shoe size of 42 EU, a progression starting at 3 feet and building to 5 feet across 8 wickets has worked well with U16 academy football players. However, the exact spacing of the wickets is not fixed and should be adjusted based on what produces clean movement from the athletes you work with.

The dribbles are performed by having the athlete run over the wickets at a slow and controlled pace. Despite the low velocity, the movement should be treated as a drill, requiring a high degree of intent and technical precision. The athlete must actively seek to replicate the quality of high-speed mechanics, such as a stiff foot contact and an upright posture, even at a slow pace. To clear the wickets, the swing leg must be lifted up and forwards with the foot passing just above the opposite knee. This ingrains the cyclical, front-side mechanics that define efficient sprinting. If the posture is not upright, it can be fixed with some simple cues. The athlete has to stand tall with hips forward to create the necessary clearance for their legs. Besides the technical components, the athlete finds the fundamental cadence and rhythm of top-speed running, all of this without the neural demand or ground reaction forces of actual sprinting. This allows them to accumulate a high volume of mechanically sound repetitions at a manageable cost.

As a progression, ask the athlete to continue running after the last hurdle, gradually increasing speed while trying to maintain the same mechanics. This seamless transition away from the constraints into the free environment offers a further benefit of the drill. It serves as an immediate test for skill transfer. This provides valuable, real-time feedback for both the athlete and the coach, highlighting stability of the motor pattern.

2. High-Intensity Application: Expressing the Skill

The setup here involves eight to twelve wickets spaced evenly to match the athlete's top-speed stride length. This will roughly be 6–7 feet, though this needs adjusting based on the athlete's size and stride. While you can individualise spacing by calculating stride length from a flying 10-metre sprint, this is often impractical in a team setting. In these cases, a standardised distance that provides a happy medium for the group is perfectly effective. The goal is to find a rhythm that feels fast and powerful for the majority of the athletes.

The task is then for the athlete to take a 20–30 metre run-in to reach maximal velocity and then sprint through the wickets at maximum effort, taking one stride between each hurdle. Where the dribble serves to pattern the mechanics, this high-intensity application serves to stress-proof them under the chaotic demands of maximal velocity. An athlete simply cannot sprint over a series of wickets at high speed without getting the knee up in front of the body and striking the ground down powerfully beneath the centre of mass. No amount of A-skips produces the same improvements in sprint mechanics as dribbles and wicket runs do. The environment solves the problem. Clearing the hurdles encourages a powerful downward strike into the ground directly beneath the hips, teaching the athlete to use the ground rather than push against it. The consistent spacing builds the rhythm and relaxation necessary for efficient sprinting.

A Recommendation for a More Efficient Practice

This is not about throwing out years of experience. It is about honestly questioning whether the methods are worth the time they consume. The drill-heavy warm-up is a habit in most programmes that feels necessary but rarely is.

Consider running a training block built around this progression instead. Wicket dribbles in every warm-up to prime the mechanical pattern. Wicket sprints as the primary maximal velocity stimulus. Then step back and watch what happens. A constraints-led approach does not make the coach redundant, it demands more from them. Different movement solutions will emerge as athletes interact with the hurdles. Some will be efficient, some won't. That is where the coaching needs to happen. The ability to recognise what is actually occurring and then finding the right cue at the right moment for the athlete in question requires a lot of experience and knowledge. It is far more complicated than deciding on the order of traditional sprinting drills.

This is the shift from being a drill collector to a true movement coach. And once you make that shift, you realise the truth: aside from wicket dribbles and wicket runs, you don't need any other sprint drills. The rest is noise. The ultimate goal is to achieve better results with less complexity, and for developing maximal velocity, this is the most direct path.
